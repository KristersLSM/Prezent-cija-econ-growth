01 · The puzzle

Hot countries are poor. But why?

A 1°C increase in average temperature is associated with an 8.5% lower per-capita income across countries.

02 · What's been tried

Two old approaches — neither works.

Approach 01 · Cross-section
Compare hot countries to cold countries.

The problem: Temperature is correlated with everything — institutions, disease, colonial history. Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson (2002) argue the relationship is spurious.

Approach 02 · Integrated Assessment
Add up sector-by-sector damages.

The problem: Requires modelling every channel — agriculture, mortality, conflict. Stern (2007) calls the simplifications "drastic, often heroic."

DJO's move: a third approach — within-country year-to-year variation, letting each country serve as its own control.

03 · The strategy

A country as its own control.

Country fixed effects absorb anything time-invariant. Region-by-year effects sweep out common shocks. What remains: the local, idiosyncratic temperature shock.

04 · The conceptual key

Level effect, or growth effect?

A simple framework — but the distinction matters enormously for the future.

↻ Level effect · temporary
Output drops, then bounces back.
Lag coefficients reverse — they sum to zero.
↘ Growth effect · permanent
Output drops — and stays down.
Lag coefficients persist — cumulated sum stays negative.
05 · Specification

The estimating equation.

git = θi + θrt + Σ ρj Tit−j + εit
Fixed effects

θi — country FE absorb institutions, geography, culture.

θrt — region × year and poor × year FE sweep out common regional shocks.

Identification

Tit−j — temperature with up to 10 lags, interacted with a poor-country dummy.

εit — two-way clustered errors at country and region-year (Cameron et al. 2011).

07 · The dynamics · Table 3

It doesn't bounce back.

If shocks were purely level effects, lag coefficients would reverse sign. They don't.

No lags · contemporaneous−1.39 pp ***
1 lag · cumulative−1.10 pp ***

A 1 pp annual gap sustained for just 7 years generates the entire 8.5% cross-sectional income gap per °C.

08 · Channels · Tables 5 & 6

It's not just agriculture.

Temperature shocks cascade through poor economies in three distinct ways.

A

Agriculture

−2.67 pp / °C

Lower yields, shorter growing seasons. The expected channel.

I

Industry

−2.04 pp / °C

Labour productivity collapses in heat. Graff Zivin & Neidell (2010) show 23% labour-supply drops above 38°C.

P

Politics

+4.4 pp coup risk

Hot years raise irregular-leader-transition probability by nearly its 4.5% baseline.

11 · Implications

Three things IAMs get wrong.

  1. 01 The rich-poor asymmetry is enormous. Damage functions calibrated on US or European data dramatically understate effects in low-income countries.DJO's poor-country coefficient is roughly five times the rich-country one.
  2. 02 Channels go far beyond agriculture. Industrial output and political stability are first-order — and absent from most current IAMs.The agricultural focus is a relic of older damage-function literatures.
  3. 03 Growth effects, not just level effects. If even modest growth effects compound, long-run damages are an order of magnitude larger.This is the single most consequential finding for policy.
12 · Critique · #1 · Where, not why

The poor-rich split bundles too much.

DJO tell us where temperature matters — but not why.

A thicket of correlations

Poor countries differ along dozens of dimensions: institutional quality, agricultural employment, capital intensity, technology access, urbanisation, healthcare capacity.

The coefficient could reflect any combination — and policy guidance depends crucially on which.

A static classification

A country classified poor in its first sample year stays poor for the entire panel. China in 1960 ≠ China in 2003.

As countries develop, the dummy grows stale — estimates become less reliable in later years.

13 · Critique · #2 · Core finding, soft evidence

Level vs growth — not pinned down.

The growth-effect interpretation is the paper's most consequential claim. The evidence is thinner than the prose suggests.

10-lag cumulative · no precipitation controls−1.17 pp *
10-lag cumulative · with precipitation controls−0.86 pp (n.s.)

Adding precipitation lags kills the significance. That fragility is uncomfortable — the difference between a level effect and a growth effect is the difference between a one-time hit and compounding damages over a century.

14 · Critique · #3 · Double-counting?

Coups — channel, or consequence?

The +4.4 pp coup-probability finding is treated as an independent mechanism. The causality is genuinely ambiguous.

DJO's reading
Politics as parallel channel

Heat → political instability → impaired institutions → permanently lower growth. Alongside the agricultural and industrial effects.

Our reading
Politics as consequence

Heat → output collapse → falling incomes → unrest. Politics is downstream of the GDP result, already captured by it.

Without an instrument or further structural assumptions, the direction simply isn't identified.

15 · Verdict

Our overall assessment.

★ Strong

The cleanest causal evidence available that temperature shocks reduce growth in poor countries. The within-country panel design is convincing and the result is robust across samples, data sources, and specifications.

★ Strong

The channel analysis decisively moves the debate beyond agriculture. Industrial output and political instability now have to be part of any serious damage model.

⚠ Caveat

The poor-rich classification is imprecise. The level-vs-growth distinction — central to long-run policy implications — is suggestive rather than decisive. The political channel may double-count.

→ Bottom line

A landmark contribution that reset the empirical climate-economy literature. The follow-up agenda is not whether heat matters for development — but through which precise structural mechanisms.

One thing to remember
A 1°C shock costs a poor country 1.3 percentage points of growth.

Sustained for seven years, that closes the entire cross-sectional gap between hot and cold nations — and there is no evidence it will close itself. — our reading of DJO 2012
