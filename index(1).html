01 · The puzzle

Hot countries are poor.
But why?

The cross-section is striking. A 1°C increase in average temperature is associated with an 8.5% lower per-capita income across countries.

The correlation is older than economics itself — Montesquieu wrote in 1750 that "an excess of heat made men slothful and dispirited."

But correlation tells us nothing about causation. Hot countries also have weaker institutions, colonial histories, and tropical disease environments.

illustrative — based on DJO Figure 1 (year 2000 cross-section)
02 · What's been tried

Two old approaches —
neither quite works.

Approach 1 · Cross-section
Compare hot countries to cold countries.

The problem: Temperature is correlated with everything — institutions, disease burden, colonial history. Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson (2002) argue the relationship is spurious.

Approach 2 · Integrated Assessment
Add up sector-by-sector damages.

The problem: Requires modelling every channel — agriculture, mortality, productivity, conflict. Even Stern (2007) calls the simplifications "drastic, often heroic."

DJO's move: a third approach. Use within-country year-to-year variation in temperature to identify the causal effect on aggregate output — letting each country serve as its own control.

03 · The strategy

A country as its own control.

Country fixed effects absorb anything time-invariant — institutions, geography, culture, colonial legacy. Region-by-year fixed effects sweep out common shocks. What remains is the local, idiosyncratic temperature shock.

04 · The conceptual key

Level effect, or growth effect?

A simple framework — but the distinction matters enormously for the future.

Level effect · temporary

A hot year hurts the harvest. Output drops. Next year, weather normalises and output bounces back.

Lag coefficients reverse — they sum to zero over time.

Growth effect · permanent

A hot year damages institutions, deters investment, depresses TFP. The country falls permanently behind.

Lag coefficients persist — the cumulated sum stays negative.

git = gi + (β + γ) Tit − β Tit−1 β captures the level effect · γ captures the growth effect · identified separately via the lag structure
05 · Specification

The estimating equation.

git = θi + θrt + Σ ρj Tit−j + εit

θi — country fixed effects
absorbs institutions, geography, culture

θrt — region × year and poor × year FE
sweeps out common regional shocks

Tit−j — temperature with up to 10 lags
interacted with a poor-country dummy

εit — two-way clustered errors
at country and region-year (Cameron et al. 2011)

Poor = below-median PPP per-capita GDP in the country's first year in the sample.

07 · The dynamics · Table 3

It doesn't bounce back.

If shocks were purely level effects, the lag coefficients would reverse sign and the cumulative sum would head to zero. They don't.

No lags · contemporaneous−1.39 pp***
1 lag · cumulative−1.10 pp***
5 lags · cumulative−1.04 pp**
10 lags · cumulative−0.86 pp

Poor countries · WDI growth data · cumulative = sum of all temperature coefficients.

The persistence matters. Effects compound. A 1 pp growth gap, sustained for just 7 years, generates the entire 8.5% cross-sectional income gap per °C seen in Figure 1.

The caveat. The 10-lag result loses significance once precipitation lags are added — the case for a permanent growth effect is suggestive, not iron-clad. We return to this.

08 · Channels · Table 5 & 6

It's not just agriculture.

DJO reject the narrow agricultural view of climate damage. Temperature shocks cascade through poor economies in three distinct ways.

A

Agriculture

−2.67 pp / °C

Lower yields, shorter growing seasons. The expected channel — but smaller than the industrial effect when scaled by sector size.

I

Industry

−2.04 pp / °C

Labour productivity collapses in heat. Hsiang (2010) and Graff Zivin & Neidell (2010) show 23% labour-supply drops above 38°C in exposed sectors.

P

Politics

+4.4 pp coup risk

Hotter years raise the probability of an irregular leader transition by nearly its baseline rate of 4.5%. Striking — and the source of one of our critiques.

All effects are for poor countries · rich-country coefficients are small and statistically insignificant.

Beyond the table. Export data from importing countries (Jones & Olken 2010) — much better measured than national accounts — show the same pattern.

Weather-station selection. Missing-station rates in the GHCN data are uncorrelated with economic or political conditions. The result isn't an artefact of poor data quality in poor countries.

Leads of temperature. Future temperature doesn't predict current growth — the timing pattern is what causal identification requires.

11 · Implications

Three things IAMs
get wrong.

  • 01 The rich-poor asymmetry is enormous. Damage functions calibrated on US or European data will dramatically understate effects in low-income countries.DJO's poor-country coefficient is roughly five times the rich-country one.
  • 02 Channels go far beyond agriculture. Industrial output and political stability are first-order — and absent from most current IAMs.The agricultural focus is a relic of older damage-function literatures.
  • 03 Growth effects, not just level effects. Most IAMs assume warming shifts the level of output. If even modest growth effects compound, long-run damages are an order of magnitude larger.This is the single most consequential finding for policy.
12 · Critique · #1
where, not why

The poor-rich split
bundles too much.

DJO tell us where temperature matters — but not why.

The dummy hides a thicket of correlations. Poor countries differ from rich ones along dozens of dimensions: institutional quality, agricultural employment share, capital intensity, technology access, urbanisation, healthcare capacity, baseline temperature.

The poor coefficient could reflect any combination of these — and policy guidance depends crucially on which.

It's also static. A country classified as poor in its first year of data stays poor for the entire panel. China in 1960 ≠ China in 2003. South Korea in 1953 ≠ South Korea today.

As countries develop, the classification grows stale — the estimates become less reliable in the later years of the sample.

For future work

Decompose the poor dummy into time-varying institutional and structural components.

13 · Critique · #2
core finding, soft evidence

Level vs growth —
not pinned down.

The growth-effect interpretation is the paper's most consequential claim. But the evidence is thinner than the prose suggests.

10-lag cumulative · no precip controls−1.17 pp*
10-lag cumulative · with precip controls−0.86 pp (n.s.)

Adding precipitation lags kills the significance. That fragility is uncomfortable — the difference between a level effect and a growth effect is the difference between a one-time hit and compounding damages over a century.

The DJO data simply can't rule out slow, mean-reverting level dynamics. A genuinely permanent growth effect requires either a longer panel or a structural mechanism. DJO acknowledge this — but the abstract and policy implications lean harder than the evidence does.

14 · Critique · #3
double-counting?

Coups — channel,
or consequence?

The +4.4 pp coup-probability finding is treated as an independent mechanism linking heat to growth. But the causality is genuinely ambiguous.

DJO's reading

Heat → political instability → impaired institutions → permanently lower growth. A parallel channel alongside the agriculture and industry effects.

Our reading

Heat → output collapse → falling incomes → unrest. Politics is downstream of the GDP result, already captured by it.

DJO note that "poor economic performance and political instability are likely mutually reinforcing" — but the paper still presents the political result as additive evidence for a growth-effect interpretation. Without an instrument or further structural assumptions, the direction simply isn't identified.

15 · Verdict

Our overall assessment.

Strong

The cleanest causal evidence available that temperature shocks reduce growth in poor countries. The within-country panel design is convincing and the result is robust across samples, data sources, and specifications.

Strong

The channel analysis decisively moves the climate-growth debate beyond agriculture. Industrial output and political instability now have to be part of any serious damage model.

Caveat

The poor-rich classification is imprecise. The level-vs-growth distinction — central to long-run policy implications — is suggestive rather than decisive. The political channel may double-count.

Bottom line

A landmark contribution that reset the empirical climate-economy literature. The follow-up agenda is not whether heat matters for development — DJO settle that — but through which precise structural mechanisms.

One thing to remember
A 1°C shock costs a poor country 1.3 percentage points of growth. Sustained for seven years, that closes the entire cross-sectional gap between hot and cold nations — and there is no evidence the gap will close itself. — our reading of Dell, Jones & Olken (2012)
